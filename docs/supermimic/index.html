SuperMimic learns 130k motions from 0.64 s of imagination at a time

One policy, 129,951 motion-capture clips, 8.7 hours on one GPU. The trick is not a bigger network or a faster simulator. It is a policy that practises inside a learned copy of the simulator, 0.64 s at a time, and is corrected by the derivative of its reward rather than by trial and error. This page follows the data through our implementation and shows what came out.

The library is BONES-SEED, Bones Studio's public motion-capture collection, released retargeted to the Unitree G1 humanoid. The method is SuperMimic (arXiv:2610.28480); the code is our mjlab fork, MuJoCo Warp underneath.

1. One policy for a whole library is a compute problem

Picture 4,096 robots in a simulator, each somewhere in a different clip. One is halfway through a cartwheel, one is getting up from the floor, one is waving. Motion tracking asks a single network to carry all of them through their clips. For one clip this is a solved problem; PPO does it in an afternoon. For a library of a hundred thousand clips the hard part is not the network. It is the bill for simulator steps.

With PPO, every step the policy learns from has to be simulated, and a policy that must learn 264 hours of motion needs a great many of them. The paper reports what that costs. The SONIC baseline trained for 7 days on 128 GPUs. PPO with 8,192 environments per GPU does not reach a return of 0.82 on LAFAN1, a small public set of 40 clips, within 18 h on one GPU (0.60), four GPUs (0.70) or eight (0.72).

Two words used throughout. A clip is tracked if the policy plays it to the end without falling (pelvis within 0.25 m of the reference and not tilted past the threshold), and failed otherwise. The normalised return is the tracking reward a clip earned divided by the most it could have earned, so 1 is a perfect copy of the reference; a clip that fails early scores low (section 8).

SuperMimic's answer is to stop asking the simulator for most of those steps. The simulator keeps running, but only to record what happens. A second network, the world model, learns from those recordings to predict the next 20 ms. The policy then practises inside that model: 32 steps, 0.64 s, over and over, with gradients flowing straight through the model's predictions. A critic estimates everything beyond the 32 steps. On the paper's 129,514-clip benchmark this gives 25 failed clips after 3 h on one GPU and 0 after 9.8 h on two, against 179 for SONIC's released checkpoint (its Table A.8).

This page is about our implementation of that method and the run behind the shipped policy, checkpoints/seed/model_144000.pt. One caveat before any number: the code described here gives the policy its heading cue in the goal (section 3); the shipped checkpoint, and every number and video of its run on this page, come from the earlier layout that put that cue in the observation. A retrain with the current layout is running.

Three networks. The policy turns what the robot senses into joint targets; it is the only thing deployed. The world model predicts the next state from the current one and an action. The critic guesses how much reward a state will still earn; it is what lets 0.64 s stand in for a whole clip.

For the record. The paper's rows are its own claims. Its benchmark has 129,514 clips; the shipped run's library kept 129,951, mainly because our filter keeps cartwheels, handstands and kozak dances (362 of the 437 extra clips). Three rules of ours added since (name terms off_wall and inchworm, a joint faster than 100 rad/s) remove 62 more and leave 129,889 clips for the retrain (Why a few clips fail, section 5).

Our bar is the shipped checkpoint: 144,000 iterations at 217 ms each. The wall clock to that checkpoint was 11.5 h, because the checkpoint evaluations shared the GPU; the whole 160k run took 12.75 h. SONIC's budget is the paper's description "7 days on 128 GPUs".

The ratio behind the bar: the run simulated 1.5 billion steps (144,000 iterations × 2.5 steps × 4,096 environments) and imagined 18.8 billion (143,500 policy updates after a 500-iteration warm-up × 4,096 rollouts × 32 steps). Twelve imagined steps for every simulated one. Of the 217 ms an iteration takes, the simulator uses 57; the rest is learning.

2. The simulator becomes a camera, the world model becomes the gym

One training iteration takes 217 ms and runs six stages. Two pictures help. The replay buffer is a flip-book: for each of the 4,096 robots it keeps the last 20 s of states and commands, page by page, and a reset simply starts a new book. The world model is a stunt double: it has studied those flip-books until it can play the simulator's next page from any current one, and it works for a fraction of the price.

Click a stage, or step through them, to see what moves between the parts and at what size (the shapes are those of the shipped run, 4,096 environments).

Two numbers set the rhythm. The simulator takes 2.5 steps per iteration (alternating 2 and 3) for one world-model fit, one policy step and one critic fit: an update-to-data ratio of 0.4, which is where the "twelve imagined steps per simulated one" comes from. And for the first 500 iterations only the world model learns, on a 64-step prefill of the buffer, so that the first imagined rollouts are not pure noise.

3. What the policy sees: 930 + 790 numbers in, 29 out

Put yourself in the policy's place. Every 20 ms you receive two things. The first is how your body feels: joint angles and speeds, how fast you are turning, which way is down, and the command you gave a moment ago, for each of the last 10 steps. The second is a request: where the reference wants your pelvis and joints for each of the next 10 frames. You answer with 29 joint targets. There is no camera, no map, no clock, no notion of where you are in the room.

The feeling is the observation, 93 numbers per step, 930 over the 10-step history. It is the paper's Table A.1 and nothing more.

The policy at = π(ot−9..t, gt) is deterministic. A goal encoder squeezes the 790 goal numbers into a 32-number tanh latent; the decoder reads that latent and the 930 observation values and outputs 29 targets. Its last layer is scaled by 0.01 at initialisation, so the first actions sit near the default pose.

Turn the robot and almost nothing changes

Now turn the robot 30° away from the direction the reference is facing. Which of those 93 numbers notice? None of them. Joints, gyro and gravity are all measured in the robot's own body, so a robot that drifts off heading feels exactly like one that has not. The reference numbers of the goal do not notice either: they describe the reference relative to itself. Something has to tell the policy, and that something is the last part of the goal.

Drag the yaw and watch which input reacts.

Where the heading cue lives. Without any cue our LAFAN1 return at 14k iterations fell to 0.581 (0.796 with one), almost all of it heading drift. We give the cue in SONIC's form, inside the goal: for each of the 10 goal frames, the reference pelvis orientation seen from the robot's current pelvis (6D, 60 numbers, ±0.05 uniform noise). It needs nothing but the IMU orientation. The shipped checkpoint used the earlier layout, one such 6D term for the current frame in the observation (99 numbers per step). On LAFAN1 at 14k iterations the two track equally: return 0.796 with 3 failed clips (heading in the observation) against 0.822 with 1 (heading in the goal), heading error at most 2° out to 60 s in both. The long story, with what BeyondMimic, SONIC and realmimic do, is in docs/supermimic/observation.md.

The request: 10 future frames, 730 reference numbers and 60 heading numbers

The goal is the reference's next 0.2 s, frames kt … kt + 9 (clamped at the clip end). Its first 730 numbers never look at the robot: each frame's pelvis pose, velocity and joints are written relative to the reference pelvis of frame kt − 1, the frame the robot's state is aligned with. The last 60 are the heading cue above.

This split matters later. In imagination the 730 reference numbers depend on the frame index alone: no noise, no gradient. The 60 heading numbers are computed from the imagined pelvis orientation, so the policy gradient reaches the imagined orientation through them; the policy reads them with fresh ±0.05 noise, while the critic gets them clean.

Normalisation. Observation and goal normalisers take their statistics from what the simulator produced until iteration 20,000, then freeze. Nothing imagined ever touches them. The heading frame of the reference part is the reference's yaw only, so its z axis stays vertical.

The answer: 29 joint targets, a quarter of the torque limit per unit

The 29 outputs are PD setpoints, but the gains are deliberately low: kp = reflected rotor inertia × (2π · 10 Hz)², with damping for a ratio of 2. An action of 1 pushes a joint with a quarter of its torque limit through the stiffness term, so a setpoint acts more like a torque command than a position to reach.

Exactly. Joint j gets qspj = αj aj + qdefaultj with αj = 0.25 τmax,j / kp,j; the actuator applies τ = kp(qsp − q) − kd q̇, clipped at ±τmax.

4. Where the noise goes

Noise is how training asks "what if?". What if the floor were slippery, the encoders slightly off, the last command a little wrong? Four kinds of randomness enter our training, and each enters in exactly one place. The one that confuses most readers: action noise is added only in the simulator. Imagined actions are noise-free, as in the paper's Alg. A.1. The noisy actions still reach imagination, but through the recordings: the world model is fit on them, the previous-action term of the observation is the executed action, and the histories an imagined rollout starts from are rebuilt from logged states and logged actions.

Pick a source; the boxes it enters light up, and the panel says how.

The evaluation turns all of it off: friction 1.0, no randomisation, no reset noise, no observation or goal noise, no action noise. What the policy learned under noise it is scored without.

5. The world model is a stunt double that predicts 20 ms at a time

A stunt double has to look like the actor only as far as the camera can tell. The world model has the same contract: it must move like MuJoCo for 32 steps from any logged state, under the actions the policy is about to try. It does not need to know where the robot is in the room or which way it faces; a fall looks the same facing north or east. So the model is told only what is invariant to that: 68 features of the state (pelvis height, gravity direction, velocities in the pelvis frame, joint angles and speeds), the 29 actions and the 33 context values. It answers with a 70-number increment, the change of the state written in the robot's own pelvis frame, which is then added onto the state.

The state it steps is the real thing: 71 numbers, pelvis position and quaternion, pelvis velocities, 29 joint angles and 29 joint velocities, read straight from MuJoCo's qpos and qvel. Both robots in the videos below are drawn from it directly. The model's last layer starts at zero, so before any training it predicts "no change", and the increment is scaled per dimension by the spread of logged increments.

One step.
ŝt+1 = integrate(st, nominal ⊙ MLP(normalise[features(st), at, ξ]))

The fit. 8-step windows of logged transitions, rolled from the logged s0 on the logged (noisy) actions; at each step the target is the increment that would take the model's own previous prediction to the logged state:
L = meanm ≤ 8 ‖ ym − delta(ŝm−1, sm) / nominal ‖²

What it cannot see. The goal (not an input), heading and world position (removed by the features and the pelvis-frame increment), and the raw terminations: reward, termination and observation are differentiable functions of the state, so there is no head to fit for them.

The fit is meant to teach the model to steer its own drift back: the target of step m is measured from the model's own prediction at m − 1, not from the logged state. In the videos, 32- and 128-step rollouts stay close to the simulator although the model was fit on 8-step windows. We have not ablated the loss, so the link is plausible, not shown.

6. The policy learns from the derivative of its reward

PPO learns the way a gambler does: try an action, see the reward, make the lucky ones a little more likely. SuperMimic's policy learns the way a student with a tutor does. Because the world model is a differentiable network and the reward is a differentiable function of the state, the policy can be told, for every one of its 29 outputs at every one of the 32 steps, "move this way and the reward over the next 0.64 s goes up". That is backpropagation through time (BPTT) through the model, and it is why 4,096 short rollouts per iteration are enough.

Each iteration draws 4,096 seeds from the flip-book: a logged state, its 10-step observation history, its frame and its context, chosen so that the rollout plus a 10-frame margin stays inside the clip. From each seed the policy and the frozen world model generate 32 steps, and the objective is the paper's Eq. 2: a discounted sum of imagined rewards, plus the critic's estimate of everything after step 32.

J = Σt=0H−1 γt ct rt+1 + γH cH V(ŝH, gH, ξ),   c0 = 1,   ct+1 = ct (1 − dt+1)

The factor ct is a soft "still standing": dt+1 is a smooth probability that the imagined state counts as a fall (sigmoids of the height and tilt errors around the thresholds 0.25 m and 0.8), so a rollout that is about to fall loses weight gradually instead of being cut, and the gradient can still say which way to lean.

For the record. Termination temperatures 0.02 and 0.04; V clamped to ±10; the loss is −mean J; gradients clipped to norm 1 and then element-wise to 0.1; one Adam step per iteration under bf16 autocast.

Where does the gradient actually travel? Toggle it on the diagram of one imagined step.

The reward it climbs

The reward is BeyondMimic's tracking reward without the self-collision penalty, computed on the imagined state by differentiable forward kinematics of 14 bodies: where the pelvis is and how it is turned, where each body is and how it is turned relative to the pelvis, how fast everything moves, minus small penalties for jerky actions and joints at their limits. Each tracking term is exp(−e / σ²) of a squared error, weighted, times the control step of 0.02 s, so the most a step can earn is 0.1. Every return on this page is normalised by that maximum.

The critic remembers what happens after 0.64 s

Thirty-two steps is not a motion; it is a glimpse. Whether the glimpse ends in a good place is the critic's job to say. After the policy step the rollout is detached and the critic is fit on the 32 × 4,096 imagined states, with λ-return targets: at each step, a mix of the reward actually imagined next and the critic's own estimate of what follows, computed backwards from its value at step 32. λ sets how far the target trusts the imagined rewards before it hands over to the critic.

Rt = rt+1 + γ (1 − dt+1) [ (1 − λ) Vt+1 + λ Rt+1 ],   RH = VH

Move λ to see how fast the targets stop trusting the imagined rewards and start trusting the critic. Unrolled, reward rk+1 enters R0 with weight (γλ)k and each value Vk+1 with γ(1 − λ)(γλ)k.

The critic's values are clamped to ±10, the largest possible discounted return (0.1 / (1 − γ)). The clamp rarely acts: on average 8 imagined states in 100,000 before iteration 52k, 4 in 10,000 between 52k and 100k, 1 in 1,000 after, with one log row at 2 % around iteration 114,800. The same critic also serves the simulator loop: episodes cut by the 10 s time-out or the clip end bootstrap their return with V(sT) before being credited to the reset sampler of section 7.

For the record. Critic input 900 numbers: 68 features, the noise-free 790 goal, ξ (33) and the privileged reference-pelvis pose in the robot pelvis frame (9). Fit: 5 epochs × 4 minibatches of 32,768 states, mean squared error, no target network. Run mean of the clip fraction 5.6e-4; first nonzero row at iteration 649; largest before 52k 7e-3.

7. Resets follow the return: a coach who drills what you fail

A good coach does not run the whole routine from the top every time. They find the second you keep falling and make you start there. The reset sampler is that coach. Where an episode starts decides what the flip-book, and so the world model and the imagined rollouts, are made of; so the sampler keeps a score for every second of every clip and starts more episodes where the score is low.

The library is cut into bins of at most 1 s, 1,015,872 of them for the 129,889 clips (the shipped run had 1,016,327 for 129,951). Each bin keeps a moving average of the discounted return observed from it: when an episode ends, every bin it passed through is credited the return from its first step in that bin. A reset then draws a bin with

pb = 0.5 / Nb + 0.4 · cap50[ softmax(−25 Gb / Gmax) ]b + 0.1 · pastb,   Gmax = 10

and a frame uniformly inside it. Half of all starts stay uniform, so nothing is ever forgotten. The softmax half leans hard towards low-return bins, and the cap is what keeps it sane: without it, one bin with a return 1 lower than the others gets e2.5 ≈ 12 times their weight, and a bin at 0 among bins at 9 gets e22.5 ≈ 6 × 10⁹ times, which is nearly the whole softmax: about half of all resets into one bin of a million. The cap limits any bin to 50 times the uniform share and hands the excess to the others.

For the record. Clip c gets ⌊frames / 50⌋ + 1 equal bins. Bin returns are exponential moving averages with factor 0.1; "past" is a slow moving average of the capped softmax (factor 10⁻⁴ per iteration). A reset never lands on a clip's last frame. Episodes cut by the time-out or the clip end bootstrap with V(sT); falls do not.

Twelve bins you control, among a million whose return the last slider sets. Drag a bin's return down and watch it attract resets until the cap holds it.

In the shipped run the cap was active from about iteration 18k to the end, and from about 44k the largest capped bin probability sat at its ceiling of 2.5 × 10⁻⁵ (25.5 × uniform). Without the cap it would have been 1.4 × 10⁻³ at 80k, 0.18 at 144k and 0.064 at 160k, peaking at 0.27 around iteration 141,850: one bin in a million taking a quarter of all resets. The normalised entropy of the reset distribution fell from 1.0 to 0.96–0.97.

8. Evaluation: 7 to 10 failed clips of 129,951

Note. The numbers and videos in this section come from the policy trained with the heading in the observation (checkpoint 144k), on the 129,951-clip library. The retrain with the goal layout of section 3 is running on the filtered library of 129,889 clips; this section will be updated.

The exam is the paper's (its App. F.1): every clip of the library plays once from its first frame with the deterministic policy, in waves of 4,096 environments, with friction 1.0, no randomisation and no noise. A clip fails if a training termination fires, pelvis more than 0.25 m from where it should be or tilted by more than the 0.8 threshold; its return is the summed reward divided by 0.1 × its steps, so a clip that falls early fails and scores low. One pass over all clips takes 4.4 min on the GPU alone.

Tilt is the difference of the gravity z component between the two pelvis frames. A clip of F frames has F − 1 steps. MuJoCo Warp is not bit-deterministic across runs: a repeat of the 144k evaluation gives 10 failed clips instead of 7, with the same mean return 0.9303 and 6 clips in common.

Every checkpoint of the run, scored on all 129,951 clips. Watch the two curves part ways after 52k.

Failures were not spread evenly. The named motion groups of the paper's Table A.8 are where SONIC fails most, and the shipped checkpoint fails none of them: all 22 side rolls, all 54 flips, all 102 lying and standing-up clips are tracked. The seven failures are all in the long tail of "all other": a wall run that needs a wall, two hard jumps, a kneeling and a sideways-jogging clip. What they have in common is the subject of Why a few clips fail.

9. Limits and open questions

The return declines after 52k. Mean return peaks at 0.952 and ends at 0.930 at 144k while failures keep falling. Every named group gains over that period (handstands 0.08 → 0.67); the loss is in "all other", on clips that survive, mostly in body orientation and pelvis position on long standing and upper-body clips. The untested explanation is that the return-based sampler moves its data to the hard bins late in the run, and the world model and policy follow it.

The failed count is noisy from checkpoint to checkpoint. After 104k it swings between 7 and 53; 160k fails 17 kneeling clips that 144k does not; two evaluations of 144k give 7 and 10 (6 clips in common). Choosing the checkpoint with the fewest failures over 40 checkpoints favours a lucky one. What the failed clips have in common is the subject of the note Why a few clips fail.

njmax 350 is tight. MuJoCo Warp's per-environment limit on constraint rows never overflowed in the run (0 "please increase" lines), but a sweep over the library with the training reset noise finds floor-exercise poses that need up to 381 rows right after a reset. The next run should use 400–450.

The heading cue moved; the results have not caught up yet. The paper's Table A.1 has 93 observation numbers and does not say where its policy gets heading from. The code now gives it in the goal, in SONIC's form (section 3); the shipped checkpoint and section 8 still come from the layout with the cue in the observation. The two layouts tracked equally on LAFAN1 at 14k iterations, and a BONES-SEED retrain with the goal layout is running. Why the cue is needed and where it can live is discussed in observation.md §4.

One seed, one run. All numbers on this page come from one BONES-SEED training run and one LAFAN1 run per A/B arm. Evaluation-to-evaluation noise alone is ±1–2 failed clips on LAFAN1.
